The GT-R makes me think about the difference between a car I admire and a car I want to make room for. Admiring it is easy. The silver body, dark wheels and four round tail-lights do most of the work before I have opened a door. Making room for it means thinking about a work bag, a narrow parking bay, a child who needs help in the back and a driveway that was clearly designed without a front splitter in mind. That is where this car becomes more interesting to me.

*The everyday scenes in this article are illustrative, imagined around the car's design and researched characteristics; they are not a log of actual trips or an instrumented road test.*

I don't want to spend the whole review pretending every journey is a qualifying lap. Most of the driving that matters happens at ordinary speeds, with somewhere to be and other people to consider. The question I keep coming back to is whether the GT-R would still feel special after the novelty, when the destination is work rather than a lookout. My answer is that I can see the attraction very clearly, provided I am honest about what the car asks in return.

## A Monday morning, rather than a launch control demonstration

Picture the weekday version: shirt on, laptop bag ready, already watching the time. I settle in and immediately become more aware of the car around me than I would in an ordinary hatch. The seating position, the body stretching ahead and the sense of width make the simple act of leaving the driveway feel deliberate. I like that. I also recognise that feeling deliberate is less charming when I am late.

At the first crawl of traffic, the GT-R's dual-clutch transmission is the detail I would judge most carefully. A performance gearbox is not automatically a smooth urban automatic, and low-speed behaviour varies across R35 years, servicing and calibration. I would rather leave a sensible gap and move forward cleanly than keep making tiny, impatient movements. The car's capability doesn't make congestion entertaining by itself. The pleasure would have to come from enjoying the machine without constantly asking it for a demonstration.

The imaginary coffee stop is a better little test than a drag-strip number. Can I park without worrying about the nose? Can I open the door far enough beside another car? Where does the bag go? None of these questions makes the GT-R less exciting. They decide whether the excitement belongs in a normal working week or whether I would start leaving the keys behind on busy mornings.

## The engineering is still the reason

Under the R35's appeal is a 3.8-litre twin-turbo V6, a six-speed dual-clutch gearbox and an all-wheel-drive layout with a rear transaxle. I like that it is an architectural idea rather than simply a large engine installed in a coupe. Major drivetrain components are distributed through the car, and the performance story depends on the systems working together.

For a historical baseline, the original 2007 Japanese specification was 353kW and 588Nm. Those are launch figures, not a claim about my silver example. Its year, tune and suspension specification remain unconfirmed. I don't need to guess a bigger number to enjoy the engineering, and I would rather know how a particular setup behaves than repeat an unsupported horsepower claim.

That distinction matters to the driving character. The R35 developed over a long production life, and changes to tyres, suspension or calibration can alter the experience again. I am interested in a car that feels coherent: the brakes, steering, gearbox and chassis agreeing on what happens next. An impressive engine figure means less to me if the rest of the car feels like separate projects.

## The road home is the part I look forward to

In my imagined working day, the better moment comes after the meetings are finished. There is no reason to chase a number. I leave a little space, choose a gear deliberately and enjoy the idea of the car responding as one piece. That is the GT-R's attraction for me: the sense of stored capability, rather than the obligation to use all of it.

I would expect to remain aware of its size and mass. The car's reputation for grip is not a reason to stop thinking about the surface or the front tyres. A heavy, powerful car still has to brake, turn and deal with bumps. That is why I find the suggestion that electronics remove all involvement unconvincing. Understanding how a machine puts its ability onto the road can be part of the enjoyment.

For Australian roads, the mundane patches matter. Coarse chip, repaired surfaces and abrupt driveway transitions are part of daily use. A firm setup can feel purposeful on a smooth stretch and tiring elsewhere. I wouldn't assume that a suspension mode, if retained on the actual car, can undo every tyre or spring choice. The ideal setup for me would preserve the sense of control without turning each ordinary road joint into the main event.

## Small passengers change the priorities

The presence of rear seats is one of the reasons the GT-R interests me as more than a two-person toy. It is still a 2+2 coupe, though. I would not confuse two small rear places with the flexibility of a family hatchback. Space depends on the front occupants, the passengers and any child restraint, and roof clearance is limited. A child fitting once is not the same as everybody being comfortable for a long trip.

In the illustrative school-run scene, the awkward part happens before I touch the start button. I slide the front seat forward, keep a bag out of the way and lean into the rear to help with a buckle. The long door needs room beside the car. If it is raining, I have also turned one simple task into a small exercise in keeping the cabin dry. I can picture finding this funny on a relaxed Saturday and much less funny before a morning appointment.

With children aboard, the journey I want is calm. Smooth throttle, predictable braking and time to get settled matter more than the engine's reputation. I would check the actual car's manual and the suitability of the restraint installation rather than assume the rear seats make every child-seat arrangement straightforward. That is especially important if a larger restraint changes the space available to the front passenger.

The reward I can imagine is sharing the enthusiasm in small ways: the shape, the badges, the view of the car after parking. It doesn't require loud starts or hard acceleration. The GT-R can be the interesting car in a family memory without making the passengers part of a performance demonstration. I like that possibility, while still seeing why I would choose the Audi for the less forgiving days.

## Bags, ramps and the supermarket stop

![The rear three-quarter view of my silver Nissan R35 GT-R](/media/gtr-rear.jpg)

The photograph from behind is my favourite reminder that practicality and character can pull in different directions. The broad tail and wing give the car its identity, but they don't make a hatch opening appear. A separate boot is useful; whether it suits a particular pram or awkward bag is something I would check with the actual item. I am not going to invent a successful packing test from a photograph.

The more immediate compromise is the low nose. My car's stance looks deliberate, and the dark front edge reinforces that. In the imagined supermarket detour, I would choose a forgiving entry rather than follow the closest car down a steep ramp. A few extra metres of walking are preferable to turning the visit into an argument with a kerb. These are the small habits a low car can introduce into a day.

Parking brings the coupe doors back into the conversation. I would want space to get out without folding myself around the neighbouring car, and more room again if somebody needs help reaching the back. The GT-R doesn't become impossible because of this. It becomes a car that rewards choosing the right bay, which is a different thing from being completely effortless.

## The costs I would include in the romance

Tyres, brakes, fluids and specialist attention belong in my idea of owning this car. I wouldn't describe a quiet week of commuting as evidence that a serious performance platform is cheap to run. Its consumables are substantial, and the quality and documentation of modifications matter. Knowing the hardware and service history would mean more to me than an exterior polished for a photograph.

There is a personal cost in attention too. Some days I would enjoy thinking about ground clearance and where to park. On a day full of appointments, I might simply want the less demanding option. I don't think admitting that weakens the enthusiasm. It makes the occasions when I choose the GT-R more deliberate, and lets the car have character without making excuses for every compromise.

## Why I would keep choosing it

What makes the R35 appealing to me is that ordinary use doesn't erase the design. The silver still describes the shoulders, the rear lamps still look unmistakable and the engineering remains interesting while the traffic light is red. It can make a routine destination feel like a reason to take an interest in the journey, even when I am not driving quickly.

I would choose it with my eyes open. The Audi makes the stronger argument for easy rear-seat access; a softer, taller car would make fewer demands on ramps and rough surfaces. But the GT-R offers a very particular combination of shape, mechanical purpose and emotional pull. For me, the convincing version of this story isn't that it does everything perfectly. It is that the special car can have a place in an ordinary life, as long as I don't ask the rest of that life to disappear around it.
