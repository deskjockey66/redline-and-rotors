I remembered the R35 being unveiled at the Tokyo motor show. It looked like a serious change from the Skylines I'd grown up wanting. Watching it on Top Gear later settled it: this was a car I wanted to own. I bought mine in December 2025, and the first drive gave me goosebumps. That part of the purchase lived up to the years I'd spent thinking about it.

Mine was gunmetal grey, built in December 2007 and treated here as a 2008 model. It was imported into Australia in 2025 and had 58,000 km when I recorded these details. It also had a custom tune producing **600 hp and 700 Nm**. That made the specification worth discussing properly, because a review of an untouched launch car wouldn't describe the build I owned.

## Why the engineering still interested me

The R35 used a 3.8-litre twin-turbo V6, the VR38DETT, with a six-speed dual-clutch transmission and all-wheel drive. Its rear transaxle arrangement put the gearbox towards the back of the car. The engineering was unusually ambitious for something with rear seats, and that was part of the appeal for me. The shape was recognisable, but the machinery underneath gave it substance.

All-wheel drive helped make sense of the performance, although it couldn't remove the limits of grip. I liked the complete engineering proposition: a turbocharged engine, a fast-shifting gearbox and a driveline designed to distribute the output. My first-drive reaction was emotional, but there was plenty here to keep me interested after that first reaction had passed.

## The supporting work mattered

NEKO serviced the gearbox. With the car tuned to 700 Nm, I'm glad that work is part of its history.

I regarded cooling and gearbox condition as part of the performance discussion. A modified car needed more consideration than comparing a peak number with another car's brochure. The parts supporting the output were part of what I owned and part of what deserved attention.

## The wheels changed the whole proportion

My Speed by Design FF5W wheels were black, measuring **20 × 11 at the front and 20 × 12 at the rear**. The tyres were **285/30 front and 335/35 rear**. That was a substantial footprint, and visually the rear tyre width gave the car a lot of its presence.

Tyre width alone didn't tell the whole handling story. Compound, temperature and alignment still mattered. Sidewall construction also influenced response, and a wider wheel-and-tyre package could change steering effort and the way a car followed road surfaces. The width gave this build a strong visual identity, while the tyre itself still had to suit the job. It was a deliberate part of this build, with practical consequences as well as the appearance I liked.

## More than the power figure

The custom tune makes **600 hp and 700 Nm**, but that number isn't the whole reason I wanted an R35. I remember watching the car on Top Gear years before I could own one. Buying mine in December 2025 meant more to me than winning a comparison on paper.

The supporting work is part of my car's story: NEKO serviced the gearbox, and the build has a front-mounted intercooler and a new oil cooler. The Speed by Design wheels, wide tyres and D1 brakes are part of it too. I wanted those details recorded because this is my particular GT-R, not a review of a factory-standard example.

What makes me smile is being able to use it. I've driven it to work, taken it to Cars and Coffee and fitted a child seat for a Sunshine Coast trip with my daughter. None of those journeys needed all 600 hp. They were still exactly what I wanted from finally owning the car.

## The bodywork was part of why I kept looking at it

The Top Secret V1 front bar was paired with the apron kit, including side skirts and rear pods. A Top Secret rear wing finished the bodywork. Those changes suited the broad R35 shape, especially in gunmetal grey. The black wheels kept the appearance tied together without needing a brighter body colour.

I like the factory R35, but I prefer how mine looks with the Top Secret front bar and rear wing.

## Work, car meets and a child seat

I drove it to work and attended meets such as Cars and Coffee. I also fitted a child seat in the back and took my daughter to the Sunshine Coast. Those were the experiences that made the GT-R an owned car in my life, rather than a specification I admired from a distance.

The two-door layout was the obvious family compromise. Getting my daughter into the back and tucking her into her seat took more effort than it would in a car with rear doors. The child seat made the trip possible; it didn't make access convenient. That distinction mattered more during ownership than another discussion of launch control.

I also owned a Tesla Model Y, which helped make the garage work. I sold the RS3 to make room for the Nissan. The Tesla meant I could keep a practical family option while buying the car I'd wanted for years.

## Where I landed

The strongest argument for this GT-R was the whole combination. The engine and driveline gave the car its performance foundation. The cooling, transmission work and brakes belonged in the same conversation as the tune. The Top Secret bodywork and wide black wheels gave this example the appearance I wanted.

The weakness was straightforward: a two-door performance car asked for compromises when I used it with my daughter. I accepted that because ownership meant more to me than finding the easiest transport for every trip. I remembered the first drive, the work journeys and the Sunshine Coast outing. There was enough real use behind the enthusiasm to make this a car I wanted to keep writing about.
