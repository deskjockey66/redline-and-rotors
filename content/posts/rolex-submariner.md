My Submariner was the watch that got most of my wrist time this year. It was a **116610LN**, bought from a dealer in Melbourne with its box and papers. The orange Delugs strap came later, during summer, because I wanted to brighten the watch up. That change gave a familiar black-dial diver a more personal place in my collection.

The thing I would change was the thickness. I'd have liked the case about a millimetre thinner. I kept wearing it anyway, which was an important part of the review: this was a compromise I lived with on a watch I used regularly. The Zenith's thickness eventually led me to sell it. The Submariner stayed.

## What this reference actually was

The 116610LN was the 40 mm ceramic-bezel Submariner Date, with an automatic calibre 3135. Its case had broad shoulders and substantial crown guards, which gave the watch more visual weight than the diameter alone suggested. Forty millimetres was one measurement; the outline of the case was what occupied the wrist.

The black ceramic insert and pale bezel markings made a strong frame around the dial. The rotating bezel carried an elapsed-time scale, while the date sat beneath the Cyclops magnifier at three o'clock. The large luminous hour markers and the broad hands gave the display an obvious hierarchy. It was easy to understand the watch at a glance, even with the extra date window interrupting the symmetry.

That was a design strength I appreciated. There was quite a lot happening around the edge of the watch, including the bezel teeth and crown guards, but the dial itself remained direct. The Mercedes hour hand was distinctive enough to separate it from the longer minute hand. The white markers against black gave the face most of its practical character.

## The movement was part of the appeal

The calibre 3135 was a self-winding mechanical movement with a date function and approximately 48 hours of power reserve. That reserve was shorter than the roughly 70 hours offered by the newer generation of Submariner Date. For a watch I wore often, the difference mattered less than it would in a larger rotation with longer gaps between wears.

An automatic movement used wrist motion to replenish the mainspring through its winding system. Stored energy then passed through the gear train and escapement to drive the hands. That basic mechanical process was the reason watches held my interest in the first place. I had started with an SKX007 and still liked the idea of a small machine working away on my wrist.

I didn't need the Submariner to have a display back to appreciate that. The visible experience was the dial and the case; knowing what powered it supplied another layer of interest. That connection between the visible design and the machinery underneath was enough to keep my interest.

## Wearability: substantial, even on a strap

This was where my main criticism sat. The case carried noticeable height, and I'd have preferred less of it. The broad lugs reinforced that feeling because the upper part of the watch had a strong, fairly square presence. The orange strap changed the look and the material around my wrist, but it couldn't make the steel case thinner.

A close-fitting cuff had to clear that case height before it could pass over the watch. That was the practical implication of the thickness complaint. A bracelet or strap could improve how securely a watch sat, but it couldn't remove the amount of watch above the wrist. Case shape mattered as much as the headline diameter.

## Why orange worked for me

The strap was an orange Delugs, fitted when I took the summer photograph. Most of the watch remained black and steel, so the colour had something clear to work against. The result looked deliberately brighter without asking me to change the watch itself.

The strap also shifted the visual emphasis. A steel bracelet extended the watch's metallic presence around the wrist; orange made the lower part of the combination more casual and much more visible. I liked that for summer. It was a fairly small intervention compared with buying another watch, but it made this one feel different enough to enjoy again.

There was a limit to what the colour change achieved. The dial and bezel still had the same design, and the case retained the same dimensions. That was useful to recognise because the strap solved a styling question rather than my thickness complaint. I liked the new appearance while keeping the criticism of the case.

## The verdict after actually choosing it

The strongest evidence in the Submariner's favour was how often I wore it. I could enjoy a more unusual dial or an interesting chronograph and still find myself returning to this watch. Its black-and-white display gave it a clear identity, and the orange strap added the part that felt more specifically mine.

The case remained the weakness. I would happily have taken a slightly thinner version with the same general appearance. That preference was consistent with why I later moved the Zenith on, although the outcome here was different. I kept using the Rolex despite the compromise.
