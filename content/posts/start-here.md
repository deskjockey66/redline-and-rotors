My interest in cars started with The Fast and the Furious when I was a teenager. The modified cars looked cool, and that eventually led me to an R33 GTST, my first JDM car. What I remembered most was the loud aftermarket exhaust and the turbo flutter. Those sounds stayed with me long after the initial excitement of buying it.

My first mechanical watch was a Seiko SKX007, which I still owned. I remembered looking through the clear caseback on mine and watching the movement. Having a small machine on my wrist was fascinating to me. The R33 and the Seiko were very different purchases, but both gave me a reason to pay attention to how mechanical things worked.

## What the R33 introduced me to

A turbocharger used exhaust energy to drive a compressor on the intake side. That allowed the engine to take in a greater mass of air, with fuel delivery and tuning determining how the extra air was used. It also gave a modified car a sound and response that could become a large part of its personality. My memories of the exhaust and flutter were tied to that mechanical experience.

An aftermarket exhaust changed more than what someone saw beneath the rear bumper. Sound could make the engine feel much more central to a journey, even when the route itself wasn't special. I don't have a power figure or a full modification list for that R33. What I remember is the exhaust and the turbo flutter.

## How that led towards the R35

The R35's unveiling at the Tokyo motor show later caught my attention, and seeing it on Top Gear made the idea stay with me. It took until December 2025 for me to buy mine. On the first drive I got goosebumps. There was a real line between the teenager interested in modified Skylines and the adult finally driving the GT-R he'd watched on television.

My own GT-R ended up with 600 hp and 700 Nm, alongside the other modifications recorded in its review. Those numbers belonged to the later car. They didn't need to be projected backwards onto the R33 to make the earlier ownership meaningful. The first car had mattered because it made the interest real.

## The SKX made the machinery visible

The SKX007's standard movement was the automatic Seiko 7S26. Its day-and-date display made it practical, while automatic winding supplied the mechanical connection that interested me. Wrist motion replenished energy in the mainspring, and that energy drove the hands through the movement.

The clear caseback on my own watch was the detail I remembered looking through. A standard SKX007 had a solid caseback, so I was describing the example I owned rather than claiming that display back as a factory feature. Being able to see the movement gave my curiosity somewhere to go. The watch was doing something physically understandable, even when I didn't know the name of every component.

## A diver's layout was easy to appreciate

That gave me a foundation for understanding what I later liked about a Submariner. The two watches occupied different positions in the market, but both made the time display direct and gave the bezel a clear function. I could appreciate the Rolex without pretending my first Seiko had become irrelevant once I owned something more expensive.

I still kept the SKX. That mattered more to this journal than trying to rank every watch in a permanent order of quality. The Seiko was where the mechanical-watch interest began, and the memory of watching its movement remained part of why I liked watches at all.

## Why I wanted a place to record it

Since those first purchases, I had owned the RS3 and moved it on for the GT-R. I had bought watches that impressed me in different ways, including a Grand Seiko whose polishing I loved and a Zenith whose thickness eventually put me off. The collection changed, but each purchase left me with a clearer idea of what I enjoyed.

That was why I started Redline & Rotors. I wanted to document the cars and watches I bought, with enough technical detail to explain their appeal and enough ownership detail to remember how I actually got on with them. There was no hidden agenda behind the site.

The R33's exhaust and the movement visible through my Seiko's caseback were good starting points. They were specific memories attached to actual things I'd owned. The later reviews could go deeper into performance and wearability, but those first experiences explained why I wanted to write the reviews in the first place.
