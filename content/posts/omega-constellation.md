My Constellation arrived without a strap. I bought it watch-only from a dealer through Facebook, then fitted the purple Delugs CTS strap in the photograph. Choosing that strap was part of making the watch mine, rather than an attempt to reproduce an original catalogue configuration.

It wore nicely on my wrist. It also went through a period of receiving very little wrist time this year while the Submariner became my regular choice. I could enjoy the Omega's shape and the strap combination even while the Submariner took most of the wrist time.

## The case gave it a different character

The rounded case and the way its shoulders flowed towards the strap gave the watch a softer outline than my modern sports watches. It had the broad, continuous shape associated with the vintage C-case Constellations. The lugs didn't look like separate narrow arms added to a circular centre; they felt like part of the same overall form.

That outline mattered on the wrist because the visible width of a watch was more than the dial opening. A case could carry a fairly modest face and still have presence through the material surrounding it. Here, the warm metal tone and the rounded shoulders gave the watch enough substance without needing a large rotating bezel.

I liked the balance of a relatively restrained dial inside that fuller case. The hands and applied baton markers kept the face simple, while the date window gave it an everyday function. The small star and Constellation script added identity without taking over the display. Those details were enough to make it interesting when I looked closely.

## What the dial actually told me

The face was marked automatic and chronometer, with the date at three o'clock. That described a watch built around automatic winding and a precision-testing tradition, rather than a purely decorative dress piece. The Constellation line had a long association with chronometer-rated movements, which helped explain the wording on the dial.

A chronometer designation related to testing and performance standards. It didn't establish how accurately this individual vintage watch ran now. Age, service condition and adjustment all mattered to that question. I could appreciate the original technical ambition without turning the printing on the dial into a present-day timing result.

I’ll add the precise case and movement references once they’re confirmed. The automatic date layout and the case design were the technical starting points for this particular watch.

## Why the purple strap made sense

The watch didn't come with a strap, which gave me a fairly open decision. A traditional leather choice would have changed its appearance in one direction. I chose a purple Delugs CTS instead, and the resulting colour was part of what I liked about this particular combination.

The strap put a casual material and a bright colour beside an older, more formal-looking watch head. The dial remained restrained, so the strap could be the part that attracted attention. It also gave the warm case tone a clear contrast. That made the watch feel more like something I'd chosen to wear now, rather than something I had to style as a period display.

I had also changed the Submariner to a Delugs strap. With both watches, the strap was a way of getting another version of something I already liked. The results were different because the cases and dials were different. Orange reinforced the Submariner's bold sports look; purple brought a lighter, more casual character to the Omega.

## Wearability: the whole combination mattered

My direct experience was that the Omega wore nicely. The case shape and the strap had to be considered together when explaining that. A strap that bent close to the case could help the watch sit around the wrist, while the broader shoulders still gave the watch head its own visual presence.

For someone thinking about a cuff, I'd consider the full side profile and the clasp underneath the wrist. A softer case outline could look more understated than a modern dive watch, but the strap and clasp still occupied space. The useful question was whether the complete combination worked with the clothes and fit someone preferred, rather than whether a vintage label guaranteed elegance.

I liked having the dressier watch head on a casual strap. That made the Omega easier for me to appreciate on its own terms. It didn't have to imitate the Submariner's purpose or become the most frequently worn watch to justify its place in the collection.

## The limits were part of owning vintage

I treated age and condition as part of the technical assessment. A historic water-resistance figure for a similar reference wouldn't establish the condition of the seals in this one. Likewise, an old chronometer designation didn't supply a current service history. Those were practical differences between reviewing an individual vintage watch and repeating a new-watch specification sheet.

My verdict was that the Constellation worked particularly well as a watch whose character I could change with a strap. Its shape and dial had enough restraint to carry the purple without looking overwhelmed by it. The caveat was the normal need to assess a vintage example individually before attaching confident mechanical claims to it. I liked the watch I bought, even during a year when another watch received much more of my wrist time.
