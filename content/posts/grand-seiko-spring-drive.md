I liked this Grand Seiko because the detail held up when I looked more closely. Mine was a 2024 frost-white Spring Drive, and the polishing was the thing that really got me. I loved wearing it to the office. It wore well for me and had enough visual interest to make an ordinary glance at the time worthwhile.

I eventually sold it because I wanted a Cartier more. That decision belonged at the end of the ownership story, but it shouldn't have been the whole review. The Grand Seiko had a textured dial, a distinctive approach to case finishing and a movement that deserved considerably more explanation than a note saying it had left the collection.

## The dial was the invitation

The pale textured surface gave the dial more character than a flat white finish. It had a pattern you could look into, while the applied hour markers provided a much clearer set of reference points around it. I liked that separation between the background texture and the sharply defined furniture above it.

The hands were important too. Broad polished surfaces could go bright or dark depending on their angle to the light, which helped separate them from the pale dial. Grand Seiko's approach to legibility involved the shape and finishing of those surfaces as well as contrast from colour. That interested me because the watch's decoration was also doing practical work.

The power-reserve indicator broke up the lower part of the dial. Some people preferred a completely symmetrical face; I found the indication relevant on a watch powered by a mainspring. It showed how much stored energy remained. The date at three o'clock added another practical function without asking the watch to become a busier design.

## Why Spring Drive was worth understanding

Spring Drive combined a mainspring power source with electronic regulation. Energy from the unwinding spring drove the hands and generated the small electrical supply used by the regulating system. A quartz reference and an electromagnetic brake controlled the rate of the glide wheel. That was why the seconds hand moved in a continuous glide rather than the familiar sequence of small mechanical steps.

The photographed watch was from the 9R Spring Drive family. For the 9R65, Grand Seiko specified automatic winding with manual winding available, approximately 72 hours of power reserve and a stated accuracy of ±15 seconds per month. Those were movement specifications, rather than results from a timing test of my own watch.

The mechanism was a large part of its appeal to me. I liked watches because of the machinery inside them, and Spring Drive approached the problem of keeping time differently from the conventional balance-and-escapement arrangement in my Rolex. The movement gave the watch an engineering identity that matched the attention paid to the dial and case.

## The finishing wasn't just a shiny surface

The case combined polished planes with brushed areas. Those different finishes made the boundaries between surfaces easier to see. A polished facet could reflect strongly beside a quieter brushed section, giving the case shape more definition even when the overall colour remained steel.

Grand Seiko's Zaratsu process aimed to produce flat, distortion-free polished surfaces. The result depended on preserving the geometry as well as achieving brightness. That distinction helped explain what I liked: the watch could look very polished without every part of the case dissolving into one rounded reflection.

My reaction during ownership was much simpler than the manufacturing explanation. The polish standard was crazy. It was one of the first things I mentioned when asked why I'd wanted the watch, and it remained a strength after I sold it. Changing my collecting priorities didn't make those details less well executed.

## Wearability at the office

I recorded it as a 40 mm watch, and it wore well for me. Office wear was its actual place in my routine. That mattered because it gave me a useful setting for judging the watch: I liked having the detail available close up without needing a large sports bezel around the dial.

The size of the visible dial and the shape of the lugs were both relevant to that impression. A pale dial could look quite open on the wrist, so diameter alone didn't explain its presence. The bracelet also extended the metallic finish around the wrist, keeping the case from being the only reflective part of the watch.

It was still a substantial automatic watch rather than an especially thin dress piece. For cuff clearance, the useful comparison was the whole side profile and the fit of the bracelet. I wouldn't assume that a restrained dial automatically meant a case would disappear beneath every shirt. What I could say from ownership was that I loved wearing this one to the office and found the size worked for me.

A power-reserve indicator was also useful when the watch was part of a collection. It answered a simple question before putting it on: how much energy was left? A three-day reserve gave the 9R65 movement useful breathing room between wears, while the indicator made that reserve visible. That was a better reason for the display to be on the dial than decoration alone.

The complication also made the layout less austere. I could understand preferring a face without it, particularly beside the texture of the frost dial. For me, it gave the watch a visible connection to the stored energy inside, which fitted the mechanical curiosity that had first made my Seiko interesting.

## The appeal beside my other watches

The Submariner had a bold outer frame and a direct sports-watch display. The Grand Seiko put more of its interest inside the dial opening and along the case surfaces. I liked having that different emphasis. Looking closely rewarded attention without needing a brightly coloured strap or a chronograph running.

It also connected with why my original SKX had interested me. Both made me curious about what was happening inside the case, although Spring Drive was a very different technical proposition. The Grand Seiko gave that curiosity a more refined setting, with a dial and finish that made sense in the office.

I sold it when the Cartier became the watch I wanted more. That was a change in preference, rather than a discovery that the Grand Seiko wasn't good. Its strengths remained the finishing, the dial's depth and the distinctive movement. The possible hesitation was that the polished case and open pale face still had a definite presence, despite the relatively restrained design.

For me, this was a successful watch that didn't have to become a permanent possession. I enjoyed wearing it, understood why the finishing appealed to me and eventually chose a different direction for the collection. A proper review needed to leave all of that on the page.
